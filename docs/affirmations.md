# Grow with Kindness: affirmation catalog and research notes

Generated from `backend/scripts/affirmation_data.py` (the source of truth). The sentences are original and **research-informed**: the sources shaped the themes and the cautious wording. None of them tested these exact sentences, and neither the sentences nor the game have been clinically validated for breakup recovery.

Two research ideas are kept apart:

- **Values-based self-affirmation** (reflecting on what matters to you) is the well-studied intervention (Cohen & Sherman, 2014; McQueen & Klein, 2006). The *identity and values* theme borrows its focus on personal values.
- **Repeating positive self-statements** is different, and sweeping ones can backfire for people who feel low (Wood et al., 2009). So every sentence is modest and believable: “I can”, “I am learning”, “I can give myself permission”.

## Self-kindness

- `kindness-01` I can be gentle with myself today.  
  _Editorial note:_ Self-kindness, limited to today.
- `kindness-02` I can treat myself like someone I care about.  
  _Editorial note:_ Self-kindness as a choice, not a feeling.
- `kindness-03` I can rest without having to earn it.  
  _Editorial note:_ Permission to rest; no productivity condition.
- `kindness-04` I can make mistakes and still be kind to myself.  
  _Editorial note:_ Kindness about mistakes (Breines & Chen).
- `kindness-05` I am learning to speak to myself with care.  
  _Editorial note:_ “Learning” keeps it believable.
- `kindness-06` I can be patient with how I feel.  
  _Editorial note:_ Patience instead of a recovery deadline.

Sources for this theme:

- **Self-compassion** (NEFF2003): Speaking to yourself with kindness, and remembering that hard days are part of being human, is the core of self-compassion.
- **Kindness and growth** (BREINES2012): Being kind about mistakes doesn't stop growth; it can support the wish to keep trying.
- **Self-compassion after a separation** (SBARRA2012): Self-compassion when talking about a separation was linked with less distress over time.
- **Modest, believable wording** (WOOD2009): Sentences say “I can” or “I am learning” instead of claiming the user already feels confident or healed, because sweeping positive statements can backfire for people who feel low.

## Identity and personal values

- `values-01` I can rediscover what matters to me.  
  _Editorial note:_ Values focus; suggested example tone.
- `values-02` I am more than one relationship.  
  _Editorial note:_ Self-concept beyond the relationship; no claim about the ex.
- `values-03` I can make room for the things I love.  
  _Editorial note:_ Interests and identity outside the relationship.
- `values-04` I am learning who I am on my own.  
  _Editorial note:_ Self-concept clarity as an ongoing process.
- `values-05` My values can guide my next choice.  
  _Editorial note:_ Values-guided action, one choice at a time.
- `values-06` I can lean on people who care about me.  
  _Editorial note:_ Connection as part of identity; no pressure to talk.

Sources for this theme:

- **Turning towards personal values** (COHEN2014): Values-affirmation research suggests that reconnecting with what matters to you can ease a sense of threat; these sentences borrow that focus.
- **Turning towards personal values** (MCQUEEN2006): Reflecting on important values is the most common way self-affirmation is studied, which is why this theme centres values, not praise.
- **Sense of self after a breakup** (SLOTTER2010): Breakups can blur who you are; sentences invite rediscovering identity and interests outside the relationship.
- **Self-concept clarity** (LARSON2015): A clearer sense of self went along with less breakup-related distress.
- **Modest, believable wording** (WOOD2009): Sentences say “I can” or “I am learning” instead of claiming the user already feels confident or healed, because sweeping positive statements can backfire for people who feel low.

## Confidence and small steps

  _Editorial note:_ Suggested example tone; mastery through small steps.
- `steps-02` One small step still counts.  
  _Editorial note:_ Values effort over outcome.
- `steps-03` I can try one new thing today.

- `steps-04` I am learning that I can handle hard things.  
  _Editorial note:_ Confidence as something learned.

  _Editorial note:_ Credit for showing up, not for results.

Sources for this theme:

- **Confidence through small steps** (BANDURA1977): Confidence grows from small successes, so sentences focus on single, doable steps rather than big outcomes.
- **Modest, believable wording** (WOOD2009): Sentences say “I can” or “I am learning” instead of claiming the user already feels confident or healed, because sweeping positive statements can backfire for people who feel low.

## Boundaries

- `boundaries-01` I can give myself permission to pause before I reply.  
  _Editorial note:_ A pause, not a rule about contact.
- `boundaries-02` I can choose what I look at and when.  
  _Editorial note:_ Choice about checking (Marshall 2012), no prohibition.
- `boundaries-03` I can protect my peace.  
  _Editorial note:_ Short and easy to say.
- `boundaries-04` I can say no to what doesn’t feel right.  
  _Editorial note:_ Boundaries as self-respect.
- `boundaries-05` I can give myself space when I need it.  
  _Editorial note:_ Space framed as care.
- `boundaries-06` My needs are worth respecting.  
  _Editorial note:_ Needs as legitimate, without demands on others.

Sources for this theme:

- **Limiting contact and checking** (MARSHALL2012): Monitoring an ex's online activity was associated with more distress, so sentences support choosing what to look at and when.
- **Self-kindness as protection** (NEFF2003): Taking your own needs seriously is a form of self-kindness; sentences frame space and pauses as care, not punishment.
- **Modest, believable wording** (WOOD2009): Sentences say “I can” or “I am learning” instead of claiming the user already feels confident or healed, because sweeping positive statements can backfire for people who feel low.

## Acceptance and moving forward

- `acceptance-01` I can miss someone and still move forward.  
  _Editorial note:_ Suggested example tone; both feelings allowed.
- `acceptance-02` I can let my feelings come and go.  
  _Editorial note:_ Acceptance of feelings without fixing them.
- `acceptance-03` I can accept today as it is.  
  _Editorial note:_ Present-moment acceptance; no outcome promised.
- `acceptance-04` I can give myself permission to grieve.  
  _Editorial note:_ Grief as allowed; no timeline.
- `acceptance-05` I can carry what I learned into what comes next.  
  _Editorial note:_ Growth without needing to forgive or reframe.
- `acceptance-06` I can move forward at my own pace.  
  _Editorial note:_ No deadline; the user sets the pace.

Sources for this theme:

- **Acceptance and values-guided action** (HAYES2006): Making room for feelings while still moving towards what matters is central to acceptance-based approaches.
- **Self-compassion after a separation** (SBARRA2012): A kinder relationship with painful feelings was linked with less distress over time; no deadlines are implied.
- **Modest, believable wording** (WOOD2009): Sentences say “I can” or “I am learning” instead of claiming the user already feels confident or healed, because sweeping positive statements can backfire for people who feel low.

## Sources

Each citation was checked against its DOI record.

### COHEN2014

Cohen, G. L., & Sherman, D. K. (2014). The psychology of change: Self-affirmation and social psychological intervention. Annual Review of Psychology, 65, 333–371.
<https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-010213-115137>

- **Kind:** values self affirmation
- **What it found:** Review: affirming important personal values (usually by writing about them) can reduce defensiveness and the sense of threat, with effects that depend on timing and context.
- **Limitations:** About values-affirmation exercises, not repeating sentences; not studied after breakups.

### MCQUEEN2006

McQueen, A., & Klein, W. M. P. (2006). Experimental manipulations of self-affirmation: A systematic review. Self and Identity, 5(4), 289–354.
<https://doi.org/10.1080/15298860600805325>

- **Kind:** values self affirmation
- **What it found:** Systematic review of how self-affirmation is induced in experiments; the most common method is reflecting on a personally important value.
- **Limitations:** Describes study methods and their variety; does not evaluate spoken affirmation games.

### WOOD2009

Wood, J. V., Perunovic, W. Q. E., & Lee, J. W. (2009). Positive self-statements: Power for some, peril for others. Psychological Science, 20(7), 860–866.
<https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2009.02370.x>

- **Kind:** positive self statements
- **What it found:** Repeating a sweeping positive self-statement improved mood for people with high self-esteem but left people with low self-esteem feeling worse.
- **Limitations:** Small laboratory studies with university students and one statement; it informs our cautious wording, it does not test it.

### NEFF2003

Neff, K. (2003). Self-compassion: An alternative conceptualization of a healthy attitude toward oneself. Self and Identity, 2(2), 85–101.
<https://doi.org/10.1080/15298860309032>

- **Kind:** self compassion
- **What it found:** Defines self-compassion as self-kindness, a sense of common humanity and mindful awareness of painful feelings, and introduces a scale to measure it.
- **Limitations:** A conceptual and measurement paper, not an intervention study.

### BREINES2012

Breines, J. G., & Chen, S. (2012). Self-compassion increases self-improvement motivation. Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 38(9), 1133–1143.
<https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167212445599>

- **Kind:** self compassion
- **What it found:** In experiments, a self-compassionate stance towards a weakness or mistake increased motivation to improve rather than reducing it.
- **Limitations:** Short laboratory inductions with students; not breakup-specific.

### SBARRA2012

Sbarra, D. A., Smith, H. L., & Mehl, M. R. (2012). When leaving your ex, love yourself: Observational ratings of self-compassion predict the course of emotional recovery following marital separation. Psychological Science, 23(3), 261–269.
<https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797611429466>

- **Kind:** breakup research
- **What it found:** Adults who spoke about their separation with more self-compassion reported less separation-related distress over the following months.
- **Limitations:** Correlational, with recently separated or divorced adults; it shows an association, not that affirmations cause recovery.

### SLOTTER2010

Slotter, E. B., Gardner, W. L., & Finkel, E. J. (2010). Who am I without you? The influence of romantic breakup on the self-concept. Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 36(2), 147–160.
<https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167209352250>

- **Kind:** breakup research
- **What it found:** Breakups can reduce self-concept clarity (a clear sense of who you are), and this loss was linked with emotional distress after the breakup.
- **Limitations:** Mostly student samples; describes what happens after breakups, not how to help.

### LARSON2015

Larson, G. M., & Sbarra, D. A. (2015). Participating in research on romantic breakups promotes emotional recovery via changes in self-concept clarity. Social Psychological and Personality Science, 6(4), 399–406.
<https://doi.org/10.1177/1948550614563085>

- **Kind:** breakup research
- **What it found:** Repeatedly reflecting on a recent breakup during a study went along with a clearer sense of self, which in turn predicted less breakup-related distress.
- **Limitations:** The reflection happened through research tasks, not affirmations; a small student sample.

### MARSHALL2012

Marshall, T. C. (2012). Facebook surveillance of former romantic partners: Associations with postbreakup recovery and personal growth. Cyberpsychology, Behavior, and Social Networking, 15(10), 521–526.
<https://doi.org/10.1089/cyber.2012.0125>

- **Kind:** breakup research
- **What it found:** Checking an ex-partner's Facebook page was associated with more distress, longing and negative feelings, and with less personal growth.
- **Limitations:** Cross-sectional, so cause and effect are unclear; about online monitoring specifically.

### HAYES2006

Hayes, S. C., Luoma, J. B., Bond, F. W., Masuda, A., & Lillis, J. (2006). Acceptance and commitment therapy: Model, processes and outcomes. Behaviour Research and Therapy, 44(1), 1–25.
<https://doi.org/10.1016/j.brat.2005.06.006>

- **Kind:** acceptance
- **What it found:** Describes acceptance (making room for difficult feelings) and values-guided action, and reviews outcome studies of the therapy built on them.
- **Limitations:** A therapy delivered by clinicians across many conditions; not breakup-specific and not a single-sentence exercise.

### BANDURA1977

Bandura, A. (1977). Self-efficacy: Toward a unifying theory of behavioral change. Psychological Review, 84(2), 191–215.
<https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.84.2.191>

- **Kind:** self efficacy
- **What it found:** Theory that confidence to act (self-efficacy) grows most from mastery experiences, such as succeeding at small, manageable steps.
- **Limitations:** A theoretical paper; it does not test affirmations.
